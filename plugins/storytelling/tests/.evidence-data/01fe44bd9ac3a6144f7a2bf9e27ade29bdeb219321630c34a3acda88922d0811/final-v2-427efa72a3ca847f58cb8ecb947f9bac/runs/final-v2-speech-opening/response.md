There’s a moment almost every first-time manager knows.

You’re about to give someone feedback. You’ve rehearsed it three different ways. One version sounds too harsh. One sounds so gentle that the point disappears. And one begins with, “This is probably nothing, but…”

So you postpone it.

Then the missed deadline happens again. The meeting goes sideways again. And now the conversation feels heavier—not because the person suddenly became harder to talk to, but because silence has been quietly adding interest.

If that feels familiar, it doesn’t mean you’re bad at feedback. It usually means you understand what’s at stake. You want to be honest without damaging trust. You want the work to improve without making someone feel small. Those instincts are good. They just need a structure.

Useful feedback is not a verdict on a person. It’s a clear conversation about something changeable: what happened, what effect it had, and what could happen next. Then you listen.

That’s the hopeful part: you don’t need the perfect words. You need enough clarity to begin—and enough curiosity to make the conversation two-way.

Today, we’ll practice how to do exactly that.
