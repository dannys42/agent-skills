At 4:40 a.m., Mina faced the kind of problem that makes every minute feel expensive. The freezer in her neighborhood bakery had failed, and a wedding order was waiting to be completed.

The first question was obvious: what could still be remade? The answer was not.

Mina tracked inventory by hand. The records existed, but in the pressure of that morning they could not quickly show her which ingredients and batches were available, what had been affected, or where to begin. The freezer failure was one problem. Trying to reconstruct the bakery’s position from handwritten notes was another.

That morning exposed a gap hidden inside a routine. Handwritten inventory had worked well enough when there was time to read through it. Under pressure, Mina needed a clear view of the bakery’s batches, not a search through scattered information. She needed to understand what she had before she could decide what she could remake.

Later, Mina and her brother built a simple batch tracker. It came from a practical question raised at the worst possible moment: when something goes wrong, can you see what you have clearly enough to respond?

The tracker began with the reality of running a neighborhood bakery, where plans live alongside perishable inventory and early-morning surprises. It was not inspired by an abstract theory about operations. It began at 4:40 a.m., with a failed freezer, a wedding order, and handwritten records that could not provide a fast answer.

Mina built the tool she had needed that morning.
